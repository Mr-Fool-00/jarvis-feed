### 3. RL for Multi-Agent Orchestration via Execution Traces (arXiv:2605.02801)
**Source:** arXiv · **Score:** 6/10 · Published: May 2026 (resurfaced in search index this window)

Formalizes multi-agent orchestration as **five learnable sub-decisions**, each trainable via RL from execution traces rather than hand-coded rules:

1. **When to spawn** — recognizing the trigger condition for delegating work
2. **Whom to delegate** — selecting which subagent or tool handles this subtask
3. **How to communicate** — formatting the delegation request for the target
4. **How to aggregate** — synthesizing results from multiple parallel agents
5. **When to stop** — recognizing when enough exploration has occurred vs. continuing to iterate

The paper uses RL over recorded execution traces (successful and failed orchestration sessions) to train a policy for each sub-decision. The key claim: hand-coded orchestration rules (explicit routing logic, hard-coded delegation patterns) break on distribution shift, but trace-trained policies generalize better because they learned from actual failure modes in the wild.

**Why it matters:** You've got a hand-coded orchestration pipeline for fiction chapters: chapter drafter → voice checker → continuity fixer → critic → editor. The "when to stop" and "whom to delegate" decisions are currently hard-coded, which means they break silently when the task structure drifts. The paper's taxonomy of 5 sub-decisions is a useful frame for identifying which parts of your pipeline are brittle — even if you're not training RL policies, knowing *which decision* is failing (spawn timing vs. aggregation vs. stop condition) helps diagnose pipeline issues more precisely.

**Build verdict: INFORMATIONAL** — conceptual vocabulary for orchestration debugging; no buildable skill yet. The 5 sub-decisions frame is worth keeping as a diagnostic checklist.

🔗 https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.02801
