### 1. Two Calls Beat Five Agents — Self-Refinement vs. Multi-Agent Pipelines (arXiv:2607.26922)
**Source:** arXiv · **Score:** 8/10 · Published: July 30, 2026 (submitted July 29)
**Authors:** Ashish Prajapati, Om Mohite

A head-to-head test: a simple 2-call self-refinement loop vs. Parishad (a structured 5-role multi-agent pipeline) — both run on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct across GSM8K (500 math questions) and HumanEval (164 coding problems). Numbers: direct prompting got 75% on GSM8K; Parishad with JSON inter-agent format got 45% (a 30-point collapse — JSON parsing failures); Parishad with plain text got 82%; 2-call self-refinement got 86.2% and used 7.4× fewer tokens. On HumanEval, direct prompting got 96.3% and Parishad got 66.5% — a 30-point regression on a task the model could already do well. The finding challenges the widespread assumption that role specialization in multi-agent systems scales cleanly to output quality.

**Why it matters:** You're building multi-agent writing pipelines with roles: chapter drafter → voice checker → continuity fixer → critic → editor. If each role is adding coordination cost without proportionally adding quality, you may be paying in compute and latency for structure that isn't actually improving the output. The key question this raises: *where does specialization actually earn its keep?* The paper suggests the answer depends on model size — larger frontier models (which Claude is) may benefit more from specialization than 7B local models, but the principle that "more roles ≠ better" needs testing on your actual pipeline, not assumed. The 2-pass refinement pattern (generate → critique/refine) is the minimum viable starting point before adding complexity.

**Build verdict: BUILDABLE** — A/B your fiction chapter pipeline: current multi-role structure vs. a 2-step (draft → self-critique/rewrite) loop on the same chapter. If quality is comparable, you've simplified your pipeline and cut orchestration overhead. Concrete, finite test.

🔗 https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26922

### 2. Can AI Agents Conduct Open-Ended AI Research? — Five Failure Modes (arXiv:2607.27191)
**Source:** arXiv (AI Snake Oil team — Sayash Kapoor, Arvind Narayanan et al.) · **Score:** 8/10 · Published: July 30, 2026 (submitted July 29)

The AI Snake Oil team ran "shadow evaluations": frontier agents were given the central research question from two unpublished NeurIPS 2026 papers, six days of runtime, and thousands of dollars in API compute. The original paper authors then graded the agents' outputs. Agents handled all the engineering (code, experiments) without human help, but failed to make substantive progress on the genuinely open-ended questions. Five failure modes documented (Princeton team's actual terminology):

1. **Poor judgment about the publishable bar** — agents misjudge what constitutes a meaningful contribution; both settled on their weakest surviving hypothesis and presented underpowered negative results as findings
2. **Uncreative responses to shortcomings** — when evidence accumulated that the approach wasn't working, agents added caveats rather than redesigning; in one run, an AI self-reviewer returned "Weak Reject" 10 consecutive rounds and the agent only hedged the language
3. **Ineffective backtracking from dead ends** — both agents executed local backtracking but retired their most ambitious research targets within the first 10 hours; neither fundamentally shifted strategy after that
4. **Poor resource awareness** — agents couldn't reliably track API budgets, deadlines, or how much scope remained vs. time/compute left
5. **Instruction drift** — over the course of a 6-day run, agents ignored explicit instructions about exploration time, review frequency, and paper length limits

**Outcome:** 0 / 2 produced papers that domain experts rated as publishable. Both did all the engineering. Neither had scientific judgment.

**Why it matters:** These failure modes are directly what your writing pipeline agents will exhibit on a complex, multi-chapter fiction project where "is this good?" requires genuine judgment. Failure mode 3 (ineffective backtracking) maps to a fixer agent that keeps revising a scene that isn't working rather than recognizing it needs structural surgery. Failure mode 2 (uncreative responses) maps to your critic adding "needs more tension" notes instead of calling out that the plot arc is wrong. Failure mode 5 (instruction drift) is why your agent ignores the "50 words per bullet max" rule on paragraph 8 of a long chapter. These have structural antidotes: explicit abandon-and-restart checkpoints, strategy-change triggers, and fixed-format review loops that an agent can't quietly drift away from.

**Build verdict: BUILDABLE** — Write a metacognitive self-check layer for your writing pipeline agents: after N passes on the same section, the agent must ask "am I in a dead end?" and trigger a strategy-change response. Based directly on failure mode 3 and 5.

🔗 https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27191

### 3. Anthropic Research: Claude Discovers a Real Novel Vulnerability in Post-Quantum Cryptography
**Source:** anthropic.com/research · Hacker News (id:49087091) · Simon Willison writeup · **Score:** 8/10 · Published: July 28, 2026

Anthropic researchers used Claude Mythos Preview in a ~60-hour semi-autonomous agentic workflow to find two actual, previously unknown cryptographic weaknesses:

1. **HAWK (NIST post-quantum signature candidate):** Found a novel lattice automorphism that halves the key strength — a meaningful security downgrade to a candidate still in NIST standardization. The finding required mathematical reasoning across specialized literature, not just code execution.
2. **7-round reduced AES-128:** Applied a novel "Möbius Bridge" technique. Not a threat to deployed AES — the 14-round standard is untouched — but a novel research result on reduced-round analysis.

The workflow: human researchers acted as project managers, not domain experts. They directed Claude at the problem, Claude read the papers, generated hypotheses, ran experiments, and iterated. The human contribution was goal-setting and final verification, not the cryptanalytic reasoning itself.

**Why it matters:** This is the clearest public demonstration that frontier Claude models can do *actual sustained technical research* — not just find known vulnerabilities or run benchmarks, but produce novel results in a specialized domain. The human-as-project-manager model is directly relevant to how you'd use Jarvis on your book pipeline: you set the creative goal, Claude handles the sustained execution. The gap between "Claude can write a chapter" and "Claude can maintain a coherent novel arc across 20 chapters" is exactly the same gap this research is closing on the technical side. If it works for cryptanalysis, the architecture works for long-form creative work at that scope.

**Build verdict: INFORMATIONAL** — landmark demonstration; no standalone skill to build. But the project-manager framing is the architecture. Absorb it.

🔗 https://www.anthropic.com/research/discovering-cryptographic-weaknesses
